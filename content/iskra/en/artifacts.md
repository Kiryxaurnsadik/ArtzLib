An anomalous formation (hereafter Artifact\*) is a product of anomalous fields. According to the working hypothesis, an artifact is a stabilised material carrier of an anomalous process. Its properties are directly linked to the type of anomaly. The material of the object that fell into the anomaly has the same kind of influence.

Conditions for the formation of anomalous formations:
1. A long stay of an object (organic or inorganic) in an active anomaly.
2. Restructuring of its crystalline and/or molecular structure under the action of anomalous fields and the anomalous processes within them.
3. Ejection of the object during the energy discharge phase of the anomaly (most often after an anomalous storm).

### Theory of the wave signature of artifacts
According to the professor's working hypothesis, each artifact has its own wave signature, formed by:

* the type of anomaly that produced it,
* the material of the original object,
* the degree of stabilisation of the anomalous process.

This signature is a combination of:

* electromagnetic,
* gravitational,
* psi-information oscillations.

It is by these waves that artifacts are detected with detectors designed to search for anomalous formations.

### Energy of artifacts
Artifacts, despite the stability of their anomalous properties, are not sources of infinite energy. During use, a gradual depletion of their inner anomalous core is recorded, showing up as a weakening or temporary disappearance of their effects. This is because the artifact uses up the energy potential accumulated during its formation and cannot replenish it on its own outside an anomalous environment. It is important to note that the nature and strength of the effects are not constant and vary from specimen to specimen, even within one group. The power of the effect depends markedly on the degree of stabilisation of the anomalous core, the conditions of formation and how long the object stayed in the anomalous field. In some cases useful properties are accompanied by pronounced negative side effects, the most dangerous of which is mixed ionising radiation, mainly highly penetrating gamma radiation, as well as secondary beta radiation. These types of radiation can partly penetrate even standard radiation protection kits, leading to cumulative exposure of the body during prolonged contact. For this reason, storing and transporting artifacts requires specialised lead-composite containers that shield and stabilise the anomalous radiation and prevent degradation of the surrounding human biofield.

### Conclusion

An artifact is not a static object with predefined properties. Its quality, behaviour and potential are determined by the conditions of its formation, the depth of analysis and, critically, the equipment used to detect and extract it.
